9  Weighting and other matching methods

TipYou need this chapter if…
  • you want the treatment effect for the whole population (the ATE), not just for the kind of patients who were treated;
  • caliper matching drops too many treated patients;
  • you want a second method as a sensitivity analysis, or a reviewer asks for inverse probability weighting;
  • you’re considering other forms of matching: with replacement, exact, optimal or full matching.

Otherwise, the 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching in Chapters 5–7 is a sound default.

This chapter builds on Chapters 5–7.

NoteIn this chapter

The question. Matching throws away patients who have no close match. Is there a way to use everyone, and when should you use another kind of matching instead?

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • explain how inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) balances confounders, and calculate ATE and ATT weights;
  • check the weights and the balance they achieve, and fit weighted KM curves and a weighted Cox model;
  • use the WeightIt package;
  • describe matching with replacement, exact, optimal and full matching, and choose a method for your question.

9.1 Weighting instead of matching

Matching makes the treated and untreated groups comparable by choosing patients. Weighting does it by counting some patients more than others.

Think about a patient with a propensity score of 0.2: someone like them had only a 20% chance of being treated. Out of 100 such patients, about 20 would be treated and 80 not. If we want a comparison as if treatment had been decided at random, the 20 treated patients are under-represented. Giving each of them a weight of \(1/0.2 = 5\) lets them stand for all 100. The 80 untreated patients each get a weight of \(1/(1 - 0.2) = 1.25\), which also adds up to 100.

Do this for everyone, and both groups end up looking like the whole population. In this weighted pseudo-population, treatment is no longer related to the measured confounders, just as in a randomised trial. This is inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW): each patient is weighted by the inverse (one divided by) of the probability of the treatment they actually received.

The weights depend on whose effect you want (the estimand, Section 5.6):

Estimand Weight for a treated patient Weight for an untreated patient Both groups are made to look like…
ATE \(1 / e\) \(1 / (1 - e)\) the whole population
ATT 1 \(e / (1 - e)\) the treated patients

Here \(e\) is the patient’s propensity score. ATT weights leave the treated patients as they are, and reweight the untreated patients to look like them. That’s the same target as 1:1 matching, but nobody is discarded.

9.2 Worked example: IPTW in the simulated cohort

We use the same simulated cohort as in Chapters 5–7 (Section 5.2), and the same propensity score model:

ps_model <- glm(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                data = cohort, family = binomial)
cohort$ps <- fitted(ps_model)

# ATE weights: 1/e for treated patients, 1/(1 - e) for untreated patients
cohort$w_ate <- ifelse(cohort$treated == 1,
                       1 / cohort$ps,
                       1 / (1 - cohort$ps))

summary(cohort$w_ate)
#>    Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
#>   1.067   1.377   1.684   1.999   2.253  11.676

9.2.1 Step 1: look at the weights

The weights range from about 1.1 to 11.7. The largest weight belongs to this patient:

cohort[which.max(cohort$w_ate), c("age", "female", "diabetes", "bmi",
                                  "smoker", "treated", "ps", "w_ate")]
#>    age female diabetes  bmi smoker treated         ps    w_ate
#> 15  40      1        0 25.9      0       1 0.08564543 11.67605

A 40-year-old non-smoking woman without diabetes had only a 9% chance of being treated, but was treated. She stands in for about 12 patients like her, most of whom weren’t treated. A few large weights are normal. But when a handful of patients carry very large weights, the results depend heavily on them, and the estimate becomes imprecise.

A useful summary is the effective sample size (ESS): roughly, how many equally weighted patients the weighted sample is worth. bal.tab() reports it in the next step. Here, the 849 treated patients are worth about 673 after weighting, which is fine. An ESS that is much smaller than the group size is a warning sign.

9.2.2 Step 2: check balance

bal.tab() checks balance in the weighted sample, just as it did after matching:

bal.tab(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
        data = cohort, weights = "w_ate",
        s.d.denom = "pooled", binary = "std",
        thresholds = c(m = 0.1))

#> bmi      Contin.  -0.0083 Balanced, <0.1

TipHow to read this output
  • Diff.Adj: the SMD in the weighted data. Every value should be close to 0 (|SMD| < 0.1).
  • Effective sample sizes: the size of each group before (Unadjusted) and after (Adjusted) weighting.

Every SMD is now very close to zero, and every patient has been kept.

9.2.3 Step 3: weighted KM curves

survfit() and survfit2() accept a weights argument. The weighted KM curves estimate what survival would have been if everyone had been treated, and if no-one had:

survfit2(Surv(time, status) ~ treated, data = cohort, weights = w_ate) |>
  ggsurvfit(linewidth = 1) +
  scale_ggsurvfit(x_scales = list(breaks = 0:8)) +
  labs(x = "Years")

9.2.4 Step 4: weighted Cox model

cox_ate <- coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated, data = cohort,
                 weights = w_ate, robust = TRUE)
summary(cox_ate)$conf.int
#>         exp(coef) exp(-coef) lower .95 upper .95
#> treated 0.6964938   1.435763 0.5855802 0.8284152

With weights, you need a robust (sandwich) standard error. An ordinary standard error would treat a weight of 5 as five real patients, and the confidence interval would be far too narrow. Recent versions of the survival package use a robust standard error automatically when the weights aren’t all 0 or 1, but writing robust = TRUE makes it explicit, and protects you in older versions and in other software.

The HR is 0.70 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.83). Like the matched model in Chapter 7, this is a marginal HR, but now for the whole population rather than for the treated. Its true value, worked out by the same brute-force simulation as in Chapter 7, is about 0.73, well within the confidence interval.

ATT weights give an estimate for the same target as 1:1 matching:

cohort$w_att <- ifelse(cohort$treated == 1,
                       1,
                       cohort$ps / (1 - cohort$ps))

coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated, data = cohort,
      weights = w_att, robust = TRUE) |>
  broom::tidy(exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high)
#> # A tibble: 1 x 4
#>   term    estimate conf.low conf.high
#>   <chr>      <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 treated    0.701    0.590     0.832

HR 0.70 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.83), compared with 0.68 from matching, but now without dropping the 135 treated patients who had no match.

9.3 Extreme weights: stabilising and trimming

IPTW has a weak spot. A treated patient with a propensity score of 0.01 gets a weight of 100. A few patients like that can dominate the analysis. Large weights are a sign of poor overlap: patients who were almost certain to get (or not get) treatment, with hardly anyone comparable in the other group. Two common remedies:

  • Stabilised weights. Multiply each weight by the overall proportion of patients who got the treatment they received. The weights then average about 1 and add up to about the real sample size. The HR doesn’t change, but the weights are easier to inspect.
  • Excluding patients with extreme scores (for example, propensity scores below 0.05 or above 0.95). This removes the patients who have no comparable counterparts, but it changes who the result applies to, so say who was excluded.
  • Capping (truncating) the weights, for example at their 99th percentile. Everyone is kept and the estimate is more stable, but the capped weights may no longer balance the groups, which can bring back bias. The app below shows how badly this can go wrong.

Whichever you do, check balance again afterwards, and report the range of the weights or the ESS alongside an IPTW analysis.

9.4 Interactive app: matching and weighting side by side

This app uses the same simulated patients as the matching app in Chapter 5 (Section 5.7): two confounders, age and severity, and a continuous outcome score with a known treatment effect. Here it compares matching with IPTW (ATE weights). In this simulation, the treatment has the same effect for everyone, so the ATT and the ATE are the same, and both methods are aiming at the true effect.

In the scatter plot, each dot is a patient. Tick Size dots by weight to see the weighted pseudo-population: untreated patients who look like the treated ones, and treated patients who look like the untreated ones, become big.

TipThings to try

For each one, predict first, then pick the scenario and check.

  1. On the Default scenario, look at the scatter plot with and without Size dots by weight. Predict: which patients get the biggest weights? How do matching and weighting compare?
  2. Predict: what happens to the weights, and to the IPTW estimate, when confounding is strong and the groups barely overlap? Then pick Strong confounding.
  3. Predict: will capping the weights at 10 help? Then pick Strong confounding, weights capped at 10.
  4. Predict: if severity isn’t measured, can weighting succeed where matching fails? Then pick Severity unmeasured.
NoteAnswers: what you should see
  1. The biggest dots are untreated patients in the top right (older, more severe: the kind of patient usually treated) and treated patients in the bottom left. They’re rare in their group, so each stands in for many similar patients. Both methods balance age and severity (|SMD| about 0.1 or less) and get close to the true effect of +2: matching gives +1.82, using 245 of the 354 treated patients, and IPTW gives +1.60, using everyone. The largest weight is about 15.
  2. With strong confounding, the groups barely overlap. The largest weight is now 57, and the effective sample size of the treated group drops to 122 of 355. Weighting fails to balance severity (SMD 0.26), and the IPTW estimate is −0.50, far from +2. Matching does better (+1.34), but only by dropping more than half of the treated patients (167 of 355 kept). When overlap is poor, no method can estimate the effect for the patients who have no comparable counterparts.
  3. Not here. Capping makes the weights more stable (ESS rises to 204), but the capped weights no longer balance the groups (SMDs 0.30 and 0.38), and the estimate gets worse (−3.01). Capping trades bias for precision, so always check balance after capping or trimming, and report it.
  4. No. Age is balanced by both methods, but severity’s SMD stays at about 0.8. Matching gives −1.03 and IPTW −1.50, when the truth is +2. Weighting, like matching, can only balance what you measured.

9.5 In practice: the WeightIt package

9.6 Other kinds of matching

Chapter 6 used 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement, with a caliper. MatchIt offers several variations. Here are the ones you’re most likely to meet.

9.6.1 Matching with replacement

With replace = TRUE, each control can be matched to more than one treated patient. Good controls are no longer “used up”, so more treated patients find a close match:

m_rep <- matchit(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                 data = cohort, method = "nearest",
                 link = "linear.logit", caliper = 0.2,
                 replace = TRUE)
summary(m_rep, un = FALSE)$nn
#>                 Control Treated
#> All (ESS)     1151.0000     849
#> All           1151.0000     849
#> Matched (ESS)  282.4489     844
#> Matched        466.0000     844
#> Unmatched      685.0000       5
#> Discarded        0.0000       0

Now 844 of the 849 treated patients are matched, but to only 466 different controls. Some controls are used several times, so the matching weights are no longer all 1. Check balance, then use the weights in the analysis:

bal.tab(m_rep, binary = "std", thresholds = c(m = 0.1))
#> Balance Measures
#>              Type Diff.Adj        M.Threshold
#> distance Distance   0.0006     Balanced, <0.1
#> age       Contin.   0.0739     Balanced, <0.1
#> female     Binary   0.0121     Balanced, <0.1
#> diabetes   Binary  -0.0075     Balanced, <0.1
#> bmi       Contin.  -0.1181 Not Balanced, >0.1
#> smoker     Binary   0.0812     Balanced, <0.1
#> 
#> Balance tally for mean differences
#>                    count
#> Balanced, <0.1         5
#> Not Balanced, >0.1     1
#> 
#> Variable with the greatest mean difference
#>  Variable Diff.Adj        M.Threshold
#>       bmi  -0.1181 Not Balanced, >0.1
#> 
#> Sample sizes
#>                      Control Treated
#> All                  1151.       849
#> Matched (ESS)         282.45     844
#> Matched (Unweighted)  466.       844
#> Unmatched             685.         5
md_rep <- match.data(m_rep)
summary(md_rep$weights[md_rep$treated == 0])
#>    Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
#>  0.5521  0.5521  0.5521  1.0000  1.1043  6.6256
coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated, data = md_rep,
      weights = weights, robust = TRUE) |>
  broom::tidy(exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high)
#> # A tibble: 1 x 4
#>   term    estimate conf.low conf.high
#>   <chr>      <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 treated    0.669    0.541     0.827

The cost is precision: the 466 controls, unevenly weighted, are worth only about 280 equally weighted patients (the ESS in the matching summary). Balance can also suffer when the same few controls are reused. Here, BMI ends up just over the threshold (SMD 0.12).

9.6.2 Exact matching on key variables

exact = ~ diabetes only allows matches between patients with the same diabetes status, while still matching on the propensity score within that group. It’s useful for one or two variables that must match exactly, for example sex in a sex-specific outcome, or study site:

m_ex <- matchit(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                data = cohort, method = "nearest",
                link = "linear.logit", caliper = 0.2,
                exact = ~ diabetes)
summary(m_ex, un = FALSE)$nn
#>               Control Treated
#> All (ESS)        1151     849
#> All              1151     849
#> Matched (ESS)     706     706
#> Matched           706     706
#> Unmatched         445     143
#> Discarded           0       0

Here 706 treated patients are matched, a few fewer than without the exact condition (714).

9.6.3 Optimal and full matching

Both need the optmatch package (install.packages("optmatch")).

  • Optimal pair matching (method = "optimal"). Nearest-neighbour matching is greedy: it takes treated patients one at a time, so an early match can use up a control that would have suited a later patient better. Optimal matching chooses all the pairs together, to minimise the total distance. Results are usually similar to nearest-neighbour matching.
  • Full matching (method = "full"). Every patient is placed in a matched set containing at least one treated and one untreated patient. A set might have one treated patient and five controls, or three treated patients and one control. Nobody is discarded, and with weights it can target the ATE or the ATT. It often gives the best balance of all the matching methods.
m_full <- matchit(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                  data = cohort, method = "full",
                  link = "linear.logit", estimand = "ATE")

The analysis afterwards is the same as for matching with replacement: use the weights, with a robust standard error clustered on subclass.

9.7 Choosing a method

Method Estimand Who is kept Weights in the analysis? Good when
1:1 nearest neighbour, caliper (Chapters 6–7) ATT (matched treated) Matched pairs only No (all 1) A simple, transparent default
Variable ratio (ratio = 2, Exercise 6.1) ATT Matched sets Yes There are many more controls than treated patients
With replacement ATT Matched treated; reused controls Yes Good controls are scarce
Exact on key variables ATT Matched pairs No A variable must match exactly
Full matching ATE or ATT Everyone Yes You want good balance without losing anyone
IPTW ATE or ATT Everyone Yes You want the ATE, or a second method

Whatever you choose, the workflow is the same: settle the design and check balance before looking at outcomes, use the weights in the outcome model, and use robust standard errors. It’s good practice to choose one method in advance as the main analysis, and report another as a sensitivity analysis. If they broadly agree, as they do here (0.68 matched, 0.70 weighted), you can be more confident in the result.

9.8 Summary

ImportantKey points
  • IPTW weights each patient by one over the probability of the treatment they received, creating a pseudo-population in which treatment is unrelated to the measured confounders. Nobody is discarded.
  • ATE weights (\(1/e\), \(1/(1-e)\)) target the whole population. ATT weights (1, \(e/(1-e)\)) target the treated, like 1:1 matching.
  • Check the weights (range, ESS) and the balance, use the weights in KM curves and Cox models, and always use robust standard errors.
  • Very large weights signal poor overlap. Stabilise or trim them, and report what you did.
  • MatchIt also offers matching with replacement, exact matching, and optimal and full matching. Most of these need the matching weights in the analysis.
  • Choose a main method in advance, and use another as a sensitivity analysis.

Answering the question. Yes. Weighting uses every patient. In the simulated cohort, IPTW gave an HR of 0.70 for the whole population (true marginal value about 0.73), and ATT weights gave 0.70 for the treated, close to the 0.68 from matching, without discarding the 135 unmatched treated patients.
