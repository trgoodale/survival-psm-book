Appendix B — Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms used in this book. The link after each one goes to where it is explained.

Accelerated failure time (AFT) model. A regression model for survival time itself. Its effects are time ratios: a time ratio of 1.5 means survival times are 1.5 times as long. Fitted with survreg(). (Chapter 10)

ATE (average treatment effect). The effect of treatment if everyone in the population were treated, compared with no-one. Weighting (IPTW) or full matching can estimate it. (Section 5.6)

At risk (risk set). The patients who are still being followed and have not yet had the event at a given time. Only they can contribute an event then. (Chapter 2)

ATT (average treatment effect in the treated). The effect of treatment among the kind of patients who actually received it. 1:1 matching of treated to untreated patients estimates the ATT. (Section 5.6)

Balance. Treated and untreated groups are balanced when their covariates have similar distributions. It is usually checked with SMDs. (Section 5.3)

Balancing score. A score such that patients with the same value have the same distribution of measured covariates. The propensity score is one. (Section 5.5)

Caliper. The maximum allowed distance between the propensity scores of two matched patients. Treated patients with no control within the caliper are left unmatched. A common choice is 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score. (Section 5.6.2)

Cause-specific Cox model. A Cox model for one type of event, with competing events treated as censored. It compares the rate of that event among patients still at risk of it. (Section 4.2)

Censoring. When follow-up ends before the event is seen, so we only know that the event happened after a certain time. Administrative censoring: the study ended. Loss to follow-up: the patient left the study. (Chapter 1)

Cohort (observational). A group of people followed over time, where treatment was chosen in routine care rather than by randomisation. (Section 5.2)

Competing risks. Events that prevent the event of interest from happening, e.g. death before relapse. Treating them as censored makes 1 − KM overstate the risk; use the cumulative incidence function instead. (Section 4.2)

Complete-case analysis. Analysing only the patients with no missing values in any variable in the model. It’s R’s default, and can be biased. (Chapter 11)

Confidence interval (95% CI). A range of plausible values for the true quantity, given the data. (Section 1)

Confounder. A variable that affects both who gets a treatment and the outcome. Ignoring confounders can distort or even reverse a treatment effect. Confounding by indication: sicker patients are more likely to be treated. (Chapter 5)

Controls. In matching, the untreated patients who are paired with treated patients. (Section 5.6)

Covariate. A variable included in a model, e.g. age or sex.

Cox model (Cox proportional hazards regression). A regression model for survival data. It estimates hazard ratios for one or more variables without specifying the shape of the baseline hazard. (Chapter 3)

Cumulative incidence function (CIF). The probability of having had a particular event by a given time, allowing for competing events that can happen first. (Section 4.2)

Degrees of freedom (df). Roughly, the number of independent comparisons a test makes. A log-rank test of \(k\) groups has \(k - 1\) df; a test for trend has 1. The df tell R which chi-squared distribution to use for the p-value. (Section 2.6)

Doubly robust. Combining two adjustment methods, e.g. matching and adjusting for the covariates in the matched Cox model. The name means two chances to get it right: the estimate should be close to the truth if either method deals with the confounding well. (Chapter 7)

Effective sample size (ESS). Roughly, how many equally weighted patients a weighted sample is worth. Very unequal weights make it much smaller than the real sample size. (Chapter 9)

Estimand. The precise quantity a study aims to estimate, e.g. the ATT or the ATE. (Section 5.6)

Event. The outcome being timed, e.g. death or relapse. (Chapter 1)

Family-wise error rate (FWER). The chance of at least one false-positive result among a set of tests. Holm and Bonferroni adjustments control it. Compare the false discovery rate (FDR): the expected proportion of significant results that are false positives, which Benjamini–Hochberg controls. (Section 2.6)

Fine–Gray model. A regression model for the cumulative incidence of one type of event when there are competing risks. It compares the probability of the event. (Section 4.2)

Forest plot. A plot showing each estimate (e.g. a hazard ratio) as a point with its confidence interval as a horizontal line, usually on a log scale with a reference line at 1. (Section 3.3.3)

Full matching. A matching method that puts every patient into a matched set with at least one treated and one untreated patient, so nobody is discarded. (Chapter 9)

Hazard. The event rate at a given time among people still at risk: roughly, “the risk right now, given you’ve survived so far”. (Section 2.4.1, Chapter 3)

Hazard ratio (HR). The ratio of two groups’ hazards. HR = 0.6 means the hazard is 40% lower; HR = 1.5 means it is 50% higher. The percentage change is \((\text{HR} - 1) \times 100\%\). (Chapter 3)

Holm adjustment. A way of adjusting p-values when several tests are done, so that the chance of any false-positive result stays at 5%. (Section 2.6)

Immortal time bias. A spurious survival advantage that appears when patients are grouped by something that happens after time zero (e.g. “ever treated”). The group had to survive long enough to get it, so part of their follow-up is time during which they could not die. (Section 4.3)

Informative censoring. Censoring that is related to a patient’s risk, e.g. the sickest patients dropping out. Being censored then tells you something about the patient, and KM estimates are biased. (Section 4.1)

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Weighting each patient by one over the probability of the treatment they received, so that treatment is unrelated to the measured confounders in the weighted sample. (Chapter 9)

Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator. The standard way to estimate a survival curve from data with censoring. The curve drops at each event time, by the fraction of at-risk patients who had the event. Also called the product-limit estimator. (Chapter 2)

Landmark analysis. Restarting the clock at a chosen time point (the landmark), keeping only patients still followed then, and grouping them by their status at that time. One way to avoid immortal time bias. (Section 4.3)

Logit (log-odds). \(\log\{p/(1-p)\}\). It turns a probability between 0 and 1 into a number on an unlimited scale. Propensity scores are compared on this scale when matching. (Section 1)

Log-rank test. A test of whether two or more survival curves differ. It compares observed with expected numbers of events. (Section 2.4.2)

Love plot. A plot of each covariate’s SMD before and after matching. Named after the statistician Thomas Love, who popularised it. (Chapter 6)

Marginal and conditional hazard ratios. A conditional HR compares patients with the same covariate values (as in an adjusted Cox model). A marginal HR compares the groups on average, across their mix of covariates (as in a matched Cox model with only treated). For hazard ratios the two can differ even without confounding. (Chapter 7)

Matching (nearest-neighbour). Pairing each treated patient with the untreated patient whose propensity score is closest. Without replacement: each control is used only once. (Section 5.6)

MCAR, MAR and MNAR. Missing completely at random (missingness has nothing to do with the patient); missing at random (it depends only on recorded information); missing not at random (it depends on the missing value itself, or on unrecorded information). (Chapter 11)

Median survival. The time by which half the patients have had the event: where the KM curve crosses 0.5. If the curve never gets that low, the median is “not reached”. (Chapter 2)

Multiple imputation. Filling in missing values several times with plausible values, analysing each completed dataset, and combining the results with Rubin’s rules, so the uncertainty about the missing values is reflected in the CI. (Chapter 11)

Nelson–Aalen estimator. An estimate of the cumulative hazard over time. Used to include the survival outcome when imputing missing covariates. (Chapter 11)

Non-informative censoring. The assumption that censored patients have the same future risk as those still being followed. (Chapter 1, Section 4.1)

Odds ratio (OR). The odds of an outcome in one group divided by the odds in another. Logistic regression coefficients are log odds ratios. (Section 1)

Omnibus test. A single test of whether any of several groups differ, e.g. the log-rank test with three or more groups. (Section 2.6)

Overlap (common support). The range of propensity scores where both treated and untreated patients are found. Matching is only possible there. (Figure 5.1)

Power. The chance that a study detects a real effect (gives p < 0.05 when the effect really exists). Small studies, and studies with few events, have low power. (Section 2.5)

Propensity score (PS). A patient’s probability of receiving treatment, given their measured covariates. It is usually estimated by logistic regression. (Section 5.5)

Proportional hazards (PH). The Cox model’s key assumption: the hazard ratio between groups stays constant over time. It is checked with cox.zph(). (Section 3.5)

Reference group. The group that other groups are compared with in a regression model. In R, it’s the first level of a factor. (Section 3.4)

Restricted mean survival time (RMST). The average survival time up to a chosen time horizon: the area under the survival curve up to that time. It doesn’t rely on proportional hazards. (Chapter 10)

Right censoring. The usual kind of censoring: we know only that the event happened after the last follow-up time. (Chapter 1)

Risk table. The table under a KM plot showing how many patients are still at risk at each time. (Chapter 2)

Robust (sandwich) standard error. A standard error that allows for correlated observations, such as members of a matched pair. (Chapter 7)

Schoenfeld residuals. Residuals from a Cox model that show whether a variable’s effect changes over time. cox.zph() tests and plots the scaled Schoenfeld residuals to check proportional hazards. (Section 3.5)

Stabilised weights. IPTW weights multiplied by the overall proportion receiving each treatment, so they average about 1. The HR is unchanged. (Chapter 9)

Standardised mean difference (SMD). The difference between two groups’ means divided by the standard deviation. |SMD| > 0.1 is a common sign of imbalance. (Section 5.3)

Stratification (in a Cox model). Giving each group of a variable its own baseline hazard, using strata(). It is used when that variable breaks the proportional hazards assumption. (Section 3.5)

Survival function, S(t). The probability of being event-free beyond time t. (Chapter 2)

Table 2 fallacy. Interpreting every HR in an adjusted model as an effect, when the model was built to estimate the effect of one variable (e.g. treatment). (Chapter 7)

Test for trend. A test of whether risk changes steadily across ordered groups, e.g. ECOG 0, 1, 2+. (Section 3.4.1)

Time origin. When the clock starts for each patient, e.g. diagnosis or enrolment. (Chapter 1)

Time scale. The units in which time is measured from the origin, e.g. days, years, or age. (Chapter 1)

Time-varying (time-dependent) covariate. A variable whose value can change during follow-up, such as “has had a transplant yet”. Each patient’s follow-up is split into periods, one row per period, and analysed with Surv(start, stop, event). (Section 4.3)

Unmeasured confounding. Confounding by variables that weren’t recorded. No propensity score method can remove it. (Section 5.5)
