Appendix C — Further reading

Each chapter ends with a short reading list for that chapter. This page collects everything in one place, grouped by topic, with a link to the chapter where each item is most useful. All of it is free to read online unless marked otherwise.

C.1 Where to start

If you read only three things alongside this book, make it these:

  • Clark et al. (2003a): survival data, censoring and Kaplan–Meier curves, for clinicians and researchers (Chapters 1–2).
  • Bradburn et al. (2003a): Cox regression and models with several variables (Chapter 3).
  • Haukoos and Lewis (2015): a two-page overview of propensity scores and their limits (Chapter 5). (May need institutional access.)

C.2 The British Journal of Cancer survival analysis series

Four papers from 2003 by the same authors, written for medical researchers. They make a clear, connected course, and are best read in order.

Part Paper Covers Goes with
I Clark et al. (2003a) Survival data, censoring, Kaplan–Meier curves, the log-rank test, reporting Chapters 1–2
II Bradburn et al. (2003a) The Cox model and models with several variables Chapter 3
III Bradburn et al. (2003b) Choosing a model, and checking its assumptions and fit, including proportional hazards Chapter 3
IV Clark et al. (2003b) Further topics, including missing data, model validation and repeated events Beyond this book

C.3 The BMJ Statistics Notes

One-page explanations from Martin Bland and Douglas Altman’s long-running series.

  • Bland and Altman (1998): the Kaplan–Meier method, with a worked example (Chapter 2).
  • Bland and Altman (2004): how the log-rank test works (Section 2.4.2).

C.4 Hazard ratios and proportional hazards

  • Spruance et al. (2004): what a hazard ratio does and doesn’t tell you, and how it relates to probabilities and median survival times (Chapter 3).
  • Stensrud and Hernán (2020): why hazard ratios often change over time, and why survival curves can be more informative (Section 3.5). (May need institutional access.)
  • Royston and Parmar (2013): More advanced. The restricted mean survival time, for when hazard ratios aren’t constant (Section 7.5).

C.5 Common traps

  • Leung et al. (1997): More advanced. The kinds of censoring, and why the non-informative censoring assumption matters (Section 4.1). (May need institutional access.)
  • Austin et al. (2016): competing risks: cumulative incidence, cause-specific and Fine–Gray models (Section 4.2).
  • Suissa (2008): the classic description of immortal time bias, with examples from drug studies (Section 4.3). (May need institutional access.)
  • Sylvestre et al. (2006): the Oscar-winners reanalysis, a short and memorable example of immortal time bias (Section 4.3).

C.6 Propensity scores and matching

  • Austin (2011): the standard introduction to propensity score methods: matching, weighting, stratification and checking balance (Chapter 5).
  • Stuart (2010): More advanced. A thorough review of matching methods and the choices involved (Chapter 6).
  • Austin (2014): estimating and reporting treatment effects on survival after propensity score matching, including marginal hazard ratios (Chapter 7).
  • Westreich and Greenland (2013): the Table 2 fallacy: why the other coefficients in an adjusted model shouldn’t be read as effects (Chapter 7).
  • VanderWeele and Ding (2017): the E-value, for judging how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be (Chapter 7).

C.7 Weighting and other matching methods

  • Chesnaye et al. (2022): a short, clear introduction to inverse probability of treatment weighting (Chapter 9).
  • Austin and Stuart (2015): best practice for IPTW, including extreme weights and survival outcomes (Chapter 9).

C.8 When proportional hazards fail

  • Uno et al. (2014): why and how to report the restricted mean survival time alongside, or instead of, the hazard ratio (Chapter 10).
  • Royston and Parmar (2013) and Stensrud and Hernán (2020), listed above, are also relevant here.

C.9 Missing data

  • Sterne et al. (2009): a readable guide to multiple imputation and its pitfalls (Chapter 11).
  • White and Royston (2009): how to include a survival outcome when imputing covariates for a Cox model (Chapter 11). (May need institutional access.)
  • Buuren (2018): a free online book by the author of mice.
  • Pishgar et al. (2021): matching and weighting after multiple imputation, with the MatchThem package (Chapter 11).

C.10 Reporting

  • Elm et al. (2007): the STROBE reporting guideline for observational studies, with a checklist for writing up (Chapter 7).

C.11 Going deeper

  • Hernán and Robins (2020): Advanced. A free book on causal inference from observational data: confounding, study design and much more.

C.12 R package guides

The guides written by the packages’ own authors are the best references for the code. Each one can also be opened from R with vignette().

  • Therneau (n.d.): the survival package as a whole, especially Cox models: vignette("survival", package = "survival").
  • Therneau et al. (n.d.-a): competing risks and multi-state models: vignette("compete", package = "survival").
  • Therneau et al. (n.d.-b): time-dependent covariates, tmerge() and landmark analysis: vignette("timedep", package = "survival").
  • Greifer (n.d.-b): the MatchIt workflow: vignette("MatchIt"), and vignette("estimating-effects", package = "MatchIt") for the analysis after matching.
  • Greifer N. Covariate Balance Tables and Plots: vignette("cobalt").
  • Greifer (n.d.-a): the WeightIt workflow: vignette("WeightIt").
  • Sjoberg DD et al. ggsurvfit documentation: https://www.danieldsjoberg.com/ggsurvfit/.
  • UCLA Statistical Methods and Data Analytics (n.d.): free workshop materials on survival analysis in R.
