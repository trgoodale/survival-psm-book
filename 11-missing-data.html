11  Missing data

TipYou need this chapter if…
  • coxph() reports that observations were deleted due to missingness, and it’s more than a handful;
  • a confounder you need for matching or adjustment has missing values;
  • you’re wondering whether to just analyse the complete cases.

This chapter builds on Chapter 3, and on Chapters 5–7 for the section on matching.

NoteIn this chapter

The question. Real datasets have gaps. What does R do with them by default, when does that give the wrong answer, and what should you do instead?

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • spot how many patients a model has silently dropped;
  • explain the three kinds of missing data (MCAR, MAR, MNAR), and when a complete-case analysis is biased;
  • carry out multiple imputation with the mice package, including the survival outcome correctly, and pool the results;
  • combine multiple imputation with propensity score matching using MatchThem.

11.1 What R does by default

Most R modelling functions, including coxph(), quietly drop every row with a missing value in any variable in the model. This is called a complete-case analysis. In the lung data, a few variables have gaps:

colSums(is.na(survival::lung))
#>      inst      time    status       age       sex   ph.ecog  ph.karno pat.karno 
#>         1         0         0         0         0         1         1         3 
#>  meal.cal   wt.loss 
#>        47        14

Each gap looks small. But put several of these variables in one model:

coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ sex + age + ph.ecog + wt.loss + meal.cal,
      data = survival::lung)
#> Call:
#> coxph(formula = Surv(time, status) ~ sex + age + ph.ecog + wt.loss + 
#>     meal.cal, data = survival::lung)
#> 
#>                coef  exp(coef)   se(coef)      z        p
#> sex      -5.400e-01  5.827e-01  1.988e-01 -2.716 0.006602
#> age       8.693e-03  1.009e+00  1.124e-02  0.773 0.439303
#> ph.ecog   5.152e-01  1.674e+00  1.483e-01  3.474 0.000513
#> wt.loss  -1.117e-02  9.889e-01  7.548e-03 -1.480 0.138841
#> meal.cal -4.499e-05  1.000e+00  2.512e-04 -0.179 0.857865
#> 
#> Likelihood ratio test=22.34  on 5 df, p=0.0004517
#> n= 170, number of events= 123 
#>    (58 observations deleted due to missingness)

The last line of the output says it: 58 of the 228 patients (25%) were dropped, and 42 of the 165 deaths. That costs precision, and, as the rest of this chapter shows, it can also bias the results. Always check n and number of events against the size of your data.

11.2 When does missing data cause bias?

It depends on why the values are missing. There are three kinds:

Kind Meaning Example Complete-case analysis
MCAR: missing completely at random Whether a value is missing has nothing to do with the patient A sample was lost in the post Unbiased, but wastes data
MAR: missing at random Whether a value is missing depends on things you have recorded BMI is recorded less often for patients who died, and you know who died Can be biased. Multiple imputation can fix it
MNAR: missing not at random Whether a value is missing depends on the missing value itself, or on things you haven’t recorded Heavier patients avoid being weighed Biased, and no standard method fully fixes it

The names are confusing: “missing at random” doesn’t mean the values are missing randomly. It means they’re missing at random once you take account of the recorded information.

For a Cox model, one rule of thumb helps. If whether a covariate is missing depends on the outcome (death, or how long patients survived), a complete-case analysis is usually biased. If it depends only on other covariates in the model, a complete-case analysis is usually fine, just less precise.

11.3 A demonstration with a known truth

We take the simulated cohort from Chapters 5–7 and delete some BMI values. BMI is recorded less often for patients who died (perhaps their final records were incomplete) and for treated patients (perhaps they were managed at a different clinic):

set.seed(1)
p_miss <- plogis(-1.5 + 1.5 * cohort$status + 1 * cohort$treated)
cohort_miss <- cohort
cohort_miss$bmi[runif(nrow(cohort)) < p_miss] <- NA

mean(is.na(cohort_miss$bmi))                              # overall
#> [1] 0.381
tapply(is.na(cohort_miss$bmi), cohort_miss$status, mean)  # by death
#>         0         1 
#> 0.2735230 0.6152623
tapply(is.na(cohort_miss$bmi), cohort_miss$treated, mean) # by treatment
#>         0         1 
#> 0.2971329 0.4946996

38% of BMI values are now missing: 62% among patients who died, and 49% among treated patients. This is MAR: the chance of being missing depends on things we have recorded.

Compare the covariate-adjusted Cox model fitted to the full data, before anything was deleted, with the complete-case analysis:

covs <- "treated + age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker"
f <- as.formula(paste("Surv(time, status) ~", covs))

full_fit <- coxph(f, data = cohort)        # before deleting anything
cc_fit   <- coxph(f, data = cohort_miss)   # complete cases only

c(full = exp(coef(full_fit))[["treated"]],
  complete_case = exp(coef(cc_fit))[["treated"]])
#>          full complete_case 
#>     0.6622329     0.5018850
cc_fit$n
#> [1] 1238

With the full data, the treatment HR is 0.66 (the truth, which we built into the data, is 0.70). The complete-case analysis uses only 1238 patients and gives 0.50, badly exaggerating the benefit. The patients it keeps are not representative: treated patients who died are the most likely to have been dropped.

11.4 Multiple imputation

Multiple imputation fills in each missing value with a plausible value, predicted from the patient’s other information, plus some random variation to reflect our uncertainty. It does this several times, making several complete datasets that differ only in the filled-in values. Then:

  1. Impute: create \(m\) completed datasets (often 10 to 50).
  2. Analyse: fit the same model to each one.
  3. Pool: combine the \(m\) results with Rubin’s rules. The pooled estimate is the average of the \(m\) estimates. Its standard error combines the uncertainty within each analysis with the variation between the imputed datasets, so the CI honestly reflects that some values were guessed.

The mice package does all three.

11.4.1 Step 1: include the outcome, the right way

The imputation model must include everything in the analysis model, including the outcome. It feels like cheating to use the outcome to fill in a covariate, but leaving it out biases the results towards no effect.

For survival data, don’t use the raw follow-up time. Instead, include the event indicator (status) and the Nelson–Aalen estimate of the cumulative hazard at each patient’s follow-up time (White and Royston 2009). mice calculates it with nelsonaalen():

cohort_miss$H <- nelsonaalen(cohort_miss, time, status)

# The variables used for imputation: the analysis variables, status and H
imp_data <- cohort_miss[, c("age", "female", "diabetes", "bmi", "smoker",
                            "treated", "status", "H", "time")]

# Keep time in the data (the Cox model needs it), but don't use it to impute
pred <- make.predictorMatrix(imp_data)
pred[, "time"] <- 0

11.4.2 Step 2: impute

imp <- mice(imp_data, m = 10, predictorMatrix = pred,
            seed = 1, printFlag = FALSE)
imp$method
#>      age   female diabetes      bmi   smoker  treated   status        H 
#>       ""       ""       ""    "pmm"       ""       ""       ""       "" 
#>     time 
#>       ""

imp$method shows how each variable is imputed. Only bmi has missing values, and it uses "pmm" (predictive mean matching): each missing BMI is replaced by an observed BMI from a patient with similar predicted values, so the filled-in values are always realistic. seed makes the result reproducible.

11.4.3 Step 3: analyse and pool

fit_mi <- with(imp, coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated + age + female +
                            diabetes + bmi + smoker))
pooled <- summary(pool(fit_mi), exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)
pooled[, c("term", "estimate", "2.5 %", "97.5 %")]
#>       term  estimate     2.5 %    97.5 %
#> 1  treated 0.6714700 0.5627439 0.8012029
#> 2      age 1.0669140 1.0576171 1.0762925
#> 3   female 0.8032146 0.6839103 0.9433308
#> 4 diabetes 1.7038476 1.4257359 2.0362094
#> 5      bmi 1.0285074 0.9994750 1.0583832
#> 6   smoker 1.7051351 1.4359722 2.0247506
TipHow to read this output
  • with(imp, coxph(...)) fits the Cox model to each of the 10 completed datasets.
  • pool() combines them with Rubin’s rules, and summary(..., exponentiate = TRUE) reports hazard ratios.
  • estimate is the pooled HR, and 2.5 % and 97.5 % its 95% CI.

The pooled HR for treatment is 0.67 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.80), close to the full-data value of 0.66, using all 2000 patients. Multiple imputation has removed the bias that the complete-case analysis introduced.

11.5 Missing data and propensity score matching

Missing confounders cause the same problem for matching: matchit() needs complete data, so the complete cases are matched, with the same bias:

# matchit() needs complete data, so keep only the complete cases
cc_data <- na.omit(cohort_miss[, c("age", "female", "diabetes", "bmi",
                                   "smoker", "treated", "time", "status")])

m_cc <- matchit(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                data = cc_data, method = "nearest",
                link = "linear.logit", caliper = 0.2)
coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated, data = match.data(m_cc),
      robust = TRUE, cluster = subclass) |>
  broom::tidy(exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
  select(term, estimate, conf.low, conf.high)
#> # A tibble: 1 x 4
#>   term    estimate conf.low conf.high
#>   <chr>      <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
#> 1 treated    0.562    0.415     0.760

The MatchThem package matches within each imputed dataset, then pools the results (Pishgar et al. 2021). It works just like matchit(), with datasets = imp in place of data:

mt <- matchthem(treated ~ age + female + diabetes + bmi + smoker,
                datasets = imp, approach = "within",
                method = "nearest", link = "linear.logit", caliper = 0.2)

# Balance, summarised across the 10 imputed datasets
bal.tab(mt, binary = "std", thresholds = c(m = 0.1))
#> Balance summary across all imputations
#>              Type Min.Diff.Adj Mean.Diff.Adj Max.Diff.Adj
#> distance Distance       0.0754        0.0843       0.0907
#> age       Contin.       0.0325        0.0510       0.0657
#> female     Binary      -0.0377        0.0014       0.0347
#> diabetes   Binary       0.0299        0.0551       0.0857
#> bmi       Contin.       0.0096        0.0371       0.0598
#> smoker     Binary      -0.0339        0.0063       0.0285
#> 
#> Average sample sizes across imputations
#>              0   1
#> All       1151 849
#> Matched    707 707
#> Unmatched  444 142
fit_mt <- with(mt, coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treated,
                         weights = weights, robust = TRUE,
                         cluster = subclass))
summary(pool(fit_mt), exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)[
  , c("term", "estimate", "2.5 %", "97.5 %")]
#>      term  estimate     2.5 %    97.5 %
#> 1 treated 0.7470145 0.6162722 0.9054938

The complete-case matched HR is 0.56. After multiple imputation and matching within each dataset, it’s 0.75 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.91), close to the true marginal HR of about 0.73 from Chapter 7.

11.6 Practical advice

  • Look before you analyse. Count the missing values in each variable, and compare patients with and without missing data, as for loss to follow-up in Section 4.1.
  • How many imputations? A common rule of thumb is at least as many as the percentage of incomplete cases (here 38%, so about 40). Ten is fine for learning. Increase \(m\) until the results stop changing.
  • What to include. Every variable in the analysis model, the outcome (status and the Nelson–Aalen estimate), and any other variables that predict missingness or the missing values.
  • Report it. Say how much was missing, how you imputed it (method, variables, \(m\)), and compare with the complete-case analysis.
  • MNAR can’t be fixed by imputation. If values are probably missing for reasons you didn’t record, say so, and consider a sensitivity analysis that shifts the imputed values up or down.
  • When is complete-case analysis fine? When very little is missing (a few percent), or when missingness depends only on covariates in the model and not on the outcome.

11.7 Summary

ImportantKey points
  • R drops incomplete rows by default (complete-case analysis). Always check n and number of events.
  • Missing data are MCAR, MAR or MNAR. If missingness depends on the outcome, complete-case analysis is usually biased.
  • Multiple imputation fills in the gaps several times, analyses each completed dataset, and pools the results with Rubin’s rules (mice(), with(), pool()).
  • Include the outcome in the imputation model: status and the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard (nelsonaalen()), not the raw time.
  • With propensity score matching, impute first, then match within each imputed dataset and pool (MatchThem).
  • Imputation can’t fix MNAR. Report how much was missing and what you did, and compare with the complete-case analysis.

Answering the question. By default, R analyses only the complete cases. When missingness depends on the outcome, that can be badly biased: in our example, the treatment HR was 0.50 instead of 0.66. Multiple imputation, including the outcome correctly, gave 0.67, and the same idea combined with matching gave 0.75 against a true marginal value of about 0.73.
