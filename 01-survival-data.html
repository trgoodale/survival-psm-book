1  Time-to-event data and censoring

NoteIn this chapter

The question. Patients with advanced lung cancer are followed up after joining a study. Some die during follow-up, but many are still alive when the study ends. How can we describe how long these patients survive?

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • recognise time-to-event data and its three ingredients: a time origin, an event and a time scale;
  • explain what censoring is, and why ignoring it gives the wrong answer;
  • state the assumption of non-informative censoring;
  • create a survival outcome in R with Surv().

This chapter has your first live R exercises: code boxes you can edit and run in the page itself. Section 1.4.1 explains how they work.

1.1 What makes survival data special?

In survival analysis (also called time-to-event analysis), the outcome is the time until an event happens. The event might be death, relapse, hospital discharge, injury, or a machine failing. Every observation needs three things:

  1. A clear time origin. When does the clock start? Diagnosis, randomisation, the start of treatment?
  2. A clear event definition.
  3. A time scale. Days, months or years since the origin, or age.

What makes this kind of data special is censoring. For some people the study ends, or they drop out, before we see their event. All we know is that their event time is longer than the time we observed. The word comes from censorship: like a censor blacking out the rest of a document, the rest of the patient’s story is hidden from us.

ImportantCensoring is not missing data

A censored observation still tells us something useful. Suppose a patient was last seen alive and event-free after 2.3 years of follow-up. We don’t know when they will die, but we do know they survived at least 2.3 years. Throwing that information away, or treating 2.3 years as if it were the event time, biases the results. Survival methods use exactly what we know, and nothing more.

1.1.1 Types of censoring

All the censoring in this book is right censoring: we know the event happened after a certain time (to the right of it, on a timeline). It has three common causes:

  • Administrative censoring. The study ends. Patients who joined late are followed for less time.
  • Loss to follow-up. The patient moves away or withdraws.
  • Competing events. The patient dies of something else before the event of interest. Strictly, this isn’t censoring at all: someone who has died can never relapse. Treating these deaths as censored is a common mistake (Section 4.2).

Kaplan–Meier and Cox methods assume that censoring is non-informative. At any time, people who are censored must have the same future risk as people who stay under observation. If the sickest patients drop out because they’re sick, this assumption is broken. Chapter 4 has an app that shows what happens then (Section 4.1).

1.2 Why not just use the average time, or logistic regression?

  • The mean observed time is biased downwards, because censored times are shorter than the true event times.
  • Using the mean among people who had the event throws away the censored people. It is biased towards fast events.
  • Logistic regression on “died: yes/no” ignores when the event happened. It also mixes up people followed for 1 month with people followed for 5 years.

The app below makes this concrete.

function simCensoring(p, n, seed) {
  const r = makeRandom(seed);
  const rate = Math.LN2 / p.median;
  const studyEnd = Math.max(p.study, p.accrual);
  return Array.from({length: n}, (_, i) => {
    const entry = r.unif() * p.accrual;
    const tEvent = r.exp(rate);
    const tDrop = p.drop > 0 ? r.exp(p.drop) : Infinity;
    const tAdmin = studyEnd - entry;
    const time = Math.min(tEvent, tDrop, tAdmin);
    const status = tEvent <= Math.min(tDrop, tAdmin) ? 1 : 0;
    const reason = status ? "Event" : (tDrop < tAdmin ? "Censored: dropped out" : "Censored: study ended");
    return {id: i + 1, entry, tEvent, time, status, reason};
  });
}

TipThings to try

For each one, predict first: decide what you expect to see, then pick the scenario and check. Committing to a prediction, even a wrong one, makes the result stick.

  1. Predict: if every patient’s line started at 0, which patients would have the shortest lines? Then switch the time axis to Time since entry. This is how survival software sees the data.
  2. Predict: if the study lasts only 2 years, will the two naive averages move closer to the truth or further away? Then pick Short study (2 years).
  3. Predict: what happens to the naive averages when almost no one is censored? Then pick No drop-out, 10-year study.
NoteAnswers: what you should see
  1. Time since entry. Every line now starts at 0. Patients who joined near the end of the recruitment period have the shortest lines ending in a blue ✕. They were censored early because the study closed, not because of anything about their health. That’s why administrative censoring is usually non-informative.
  2. Study length 2 years. Most lines now end in a ✕. With the other settings at their defaults, about 80% of patients are censored. The naive mean falls to about 0.8 years (truth: 4.3), and the mean among those with an event to about 0.6 years. Both get worse the shorter the follow-up. The Kaplan–Meier method (Chapter 2) uses the censored patients properly.
  3. No drop-out, 10-year study. Only people still alive after 8–10 years are censored, about 13% of patients. The naive mean rises to about 3.8 years, much closer to 4.3. It’s still too low, because the few censored patients are the longest survivors. Censoring causes the bias: the less of it there is, the smaller the bias.

1.4 Survival data in R: the Surv() object

We’ll use the lung dataset from the survival package. It has survival times (in days) for 228 patients with advanced lung cancer.

head(survival::lung)
#>   inst time status age sex ph.ecog ph.karno pat.karno meal.cal wt.loss
#> 1    3  306      2  74   1       1       90       100     1175      NA
#> 2    3  455      2  68   1       0       90        90     1225      15
#> 3    3 1010      1  56   1       0       90        90       NA      15
#> 4    5  210      2  57   1       1       90        60     1150      11
#> 5    1  883      2  60   1       0      100        90       NA       0
#> 6   12 1022      1  74   1       1       50        80      513       0

The important columns are time (days of follow-up) and status. Note that in this dataset status is coded 1 = censored, 2 = dead. R accepts 1/2, but 0/1 or FALSE/TRUE are clearer, so we recode. We also turn sex into a labelled factor.

lung <- survival::lung |>
  mutate(
    status = status - 1,                              # 0 = censored, 1 = died
    sex    = factor(sex, levels = 1:2, labels = c("Male", "Female")),
    time_m = time / 30.44                             # follow-up in months
  )

table(lung$status)        # 63 censored, 165 deaths
#> 
#>   0   1 
#>  63 165

Surv(time, status) combines the two columns into one survival outcome. Censored times are printed with a +:

Surv(lung$time, lung$status)[1:15]
#>  [1]  306   455  1010+  210   883  1022+  310   361   218   166   170   654 
#> [13]  728    71   567

Almost every survival function in R takes a formula with Surv() on the left-hand side, for example:

survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ sex, data = lung)         # Kaplan-Meier, Ch 2
coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ sex + age, data = lung)     # Cox model, Ch 3
NoteGoing further (optional): other forms of Surv()
  • Surv(start, stop, event): counting-process form. Use it for time-varying covariates and delayed entry (left truncation).
  • Surv(time, event, type = "interval"): interval-censored data, where you only know the event happened between two visits.

1.6 Summary

ImportantKey points
  • Survival data record the time until an event. You need a clear time origin, a clear event and a time scale.
  • A censored observation tells us only that the event happened after the last follow-up. It isn’t missing data. It carries real information.
  • Ignoring censoring gives the wrong answer: averages of observed times are too low, and yes/no analyses ignore when events happen.
  • Survival methods assume censoring is non-informative: people who are censored have the same future risk as those still being followed.
  • In R, Surv(time, status) combines follow-up time and event status (1 = event, 0 = censored). In lung, status must be recoded from 1/2 to 0/1 first.

Answering the question. We can’t just average the follow-up times, because 63 of the 228 patients were censored, so their follow-up times are shorter than their true survival times. We need a method that uses the information in censored patients correctly. That’s the Kaplan–Meier estimator, in the next chapter.
